Introduction

This project aims to analyze the correlation between various quantitative album attributes and the winners of the Grammy Awards "Album of the Year" award.

The Grammy Awards, abbreviated as the GRAMMYs, is an award show presented by the United States Recording Academy whose mission is to "recognize excellence in the recording arts ... and ensure that music remains an indelible part of our culture" [1].

The Grammy Awards have been a pop culture phenomenon for decades, pulling in roughly 10 million views consistently for 40 years [2]. The widespread appeal of the awards has led to a commercial effect, known to be called the GRAMMY effect, which attributes large-scale commercial success, of an album, to winning a Album of the Year GRAMMY award.

Giacomo Negro at Emory University, along with colleagues, examined what happens to artists after receiving Grammy recognition. Their findings support the GRAMMY Effect: a nomination alone correlated with a jump of roughly 22 spots in Billboard peak chart position, while an actual win pushes that to about 27 [4].

Beyond their clear economic influence, the Grammy Awards hold major historical and sociological significance within the United States. In the 2006 paper Award Ceremony as an Arbiter of Commerce and Canon in the Popular Music Industry, Mary R Watson talks about how the Recording Academy positioned the Grammys as the “hallmark of peer recognition”, and that the Grammys positioned themselves, uniquely, as a mingle between "peer and popular recognition" making "them a significant arbiter of canon formation in the popular music industry".

Interestingly, the paper cites growing agendas of internal Recording Academy constituents to exert influence on award decisions formatively impacting legitimacy of genres in popular music industry. An early example of this is the first Grammy Awards (1958), which deliberately left out the high-Billboard charting rock-and-roll genre due to it being artistically 'inappropriate'. Within context, the Academy consisted of music elites (CEOs of Recording Studios, Radios) who considered rock-and-roll as a 'profane' genre.

Additionally, Watson also states that the modern Awards have transformed to a status symbol within the music industry; with an analysis of the Billboard discourse finding that majority of the text units, between Artists, refer to the idea of winning an award translating to respect and reputation within the field.

Ultimatley, the Grammys have a huge pop culture impact; posing a sociological, economical, and historical impact throughout the years. And today, the Grammy Awards voting process advocates for an unbiased decision based on merit. Specifically, the Voter's Code of Conduct states that a Voter must only be influenced by own analysis of merit, and stating they should not be suggested by "sales volume/popularity" or "company loyalties".

This project questions this merit-based judging by doing an analysis, specifically, on quantitative album attributes relevant to commercial performance, critical reception, and musical characteristics of an album before it got nominated, and won, an Album of the Year award.

Contribution to Scholarship

Motivation behind the analysis

A Voter for the Grammy Awards, formally called the Recording Academy Voting Member, casts in the "Final Voting" phase - allowing these Voting Members to vote for three Fields in the genre Fields (in which they are advised to vote in their area of expertise) plus the four categories in the General Field. (grammy award website voting)

Because Album of the Year belongs to the General Field, every Voting Member is required to cast a vote for it. This naturally introduces high variability in voting outcomes, as not all voters share equal expertise.

Looking into how one can be a Record Academy Voting Member showed that it is an invite only group. Every year, the Recording Academy invites a "class" of new members into the Voting pool. The Recording Academy doesn't explicitly describe the qualifications to get an invitation, but rather states that Voting Members include "artists, producers, engineers, songwriters, and executive[s]" as a way to "recognize and celebrate the work of their peers" (https://www.grammy.com/news/who-votes-for-the-2026-grammys)

There does seem to be a red flag here, specifically between the official Academys' Voters Code of Conduct and the sample of people they chose to invite to be a Voting Member.

It would be naive to assume that top-level record executives are unaware of the GRAMMY Effect, or that they would ignore the potential financial gain that a win can produce by voting purely on artistic merit.

While we cannot directly prove voter intent, we can scrutinize loyalty and decision-making by examining correlations between sales volume or popularity and Album of the Year winners, as well as by analyzing musical characteristics to evaluate the expertise and priorities of the voting body.

Scoping Quantitative Album Attributes

We can question the merit-based judging by observing if there is a correlation between Album of the Year winners and "sales volume/popularity".

In this case, we need metrics pertaining to both "sales volume" and "popularity". Note that the two correlate each other, when an album has higher sales volume, it is more popular.

Sales volume is a bit tricky, especially because we have to use metrics that account for the streaming era. The Album-Equivalent Unit (AEU) does just that. In 2014, the Billboard music charts - an industry standard record charts - switched from a pure album-sales metric to a AEU metric (citation needed). This new metric takes into account album-streams; with 10 song sales, or 1500 song streams contained within an album to one traditional album sale. This AEU metric, serves as a bridge between the present and the past, and seems to be an industry standard for today’s music-charts (citation).

I talked briefly about how the Grammy Awards Voter’s Code of Conduct specifically says a voter must not vote on, among other things, "company loyalty". I made a hypothetical example with a record executive, and one of their signed artists, questioning the merit of their vote. While I acknowledged a correlation between that specific example is impossible to show, since the Voting Members list is not public, we can still question the merit with respect to a Voting Members "company loyalty" through other metrics.

Pitchfork is an American online music magazine, primarily known for its album reviews (citation). In 2022 and 2023, Pitchfork averaged nearly three million unique monthly visitors (citation). In 2015, the American mass media company Condé Nast purchased Pitchfork, leading to a new creative direction and expanding opportunities for industry collaboration. Notably, in 2021, Condé Nast and Universal Music Group partnered in a joint initiative to “expand video reach for advertisers,” with the goal of allowing advertisers to access the combined “reach and influence of both companies.”

Universal Music Group (UMG) is one of the “Big Three” companies controlling a significant portion of the United States music market (citation). Notable modern artists under UMG include Taylor Swift, Kendrick Lamar, Drake, The Weeknd, Billie Eilish, and others. This direct connection and collaboration between Condé Nast and UMG prompts an investigation into whether a correlation exists between Pitchfork album review scores and Album of the Year winners. Such a correlation does not prove voter intent or label-based loyalty (and may be coincidental), but it raises the question of whether critical reviews and Grammy outcomes align more closely than expected.

Quantitative Album Attributes extends to the composition of musical characteristics as well. A song can contain quantitative attributes such as: Tempo (BPM), Key, Duration, and Loudness.

In a 2025 study titled Music Popularity Prediction Through Data Analysis of Music’s Characteristics, Jaehyun Kim developed a machine-learning model that achieved 90% accuracy in predicting song popularity based on quantitative audio features. His findings indicate that loudness, BPM, danceability, and valence directly correlate with a track’s popularity. If similar attributes are found to correlate with Album of the Year winners, it calls into question the extent to which Voting Members prioritize artistic merit - and, accordingly, the Academy’s stated mission to "recognize excellence in the recording arts".

Steps to execute the analysis

Fundementally, our analysis is three questions:

  1. Is there a correlation between an album’s Album-Equivalent Units (AEU) and its likelihood of winning Album of the Year?
  2. Is there a correlation between Pitchfork review scores and Album of the Year outcomes?
  3. Is there a correlation between quantitative musical characteristics (BPM, loudness, danceability), that are shown to give a song mass appeal, and the album winning Album of the Year?

Correlation is mathematically defined as the joint covariance between two random variables, divided by their products of their standard deviation.

\documentclass{article} \usepackage{amsmath} \begin{document} \[ {\huge corr({X,Y}) = \frac{\operatorname{cov}(X, Y)}{\sigma_X \, \sigma_Y}} \] \end{document}

A random variable is a mapping from a sample space to the real numbers. Since our analysis deals with quantitative real-valued features, we can apply the standard covariance and standard deviation formulations directly when calculating joint covariance. (citation)

The question then becomes: how do we map Album of the Year (AOTY) status to the real numbers? We define AOTY as an indicator/binary random variable, that is, 1 if album X wins, 0 otherwise.

This works, but it comes with a limitation. We are comparing continuous random features (AEUs, song characteristics, Pitchfork review scores) against a binary one. Traditional correlation formulas here flatten nuance, failing to represent the distributional differences between winners and non-winners. (citation)

Statisticians have addressed this exact scenario. For a continuous feature measured against a binary outcome, the appropriate metric is the point-biserial correlation. Our analysis therefore partitions albums into winners and non-winners, then compares the mean value of each quantitative feature across groups.

What we get is a coefficent, rpb, which ranges from -1 to 1 with extremitys indicating a perfect negative correlation, or a perfect postive correlation, respectively.

\documentclass{article} \usepackage{amsmath} \begin{document} \[ \huge{r_{pb} = \frac{\mu_{1} - \mu_{0}}{s_{n}} \sqrt{\frac{n_{1} n_{0}}{n^{2}}}} \] \end{document}

The formula essentially tells us to take the mean value of our continuous feature X (AEU, song characteristics, Pitchfork review scores) among winners, minus the mean among non-winners. We then divide this difference by the standard deviation of X, scaled by a factor that accounts for sample balance across groups. Specifically, the denominator includes the standard deviation of X multiplied by the square root of the product of group sizes divided by the total number of data points squared.

This allows us to evaluate how strongly higher feature values, X, are associated with winning.

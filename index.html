Introduction

This project aims to analyze the correlation between various quantitative album attributes and the winners of the Grammy Awards "Album of the Year" award.

The Grammy Awards, abbreviated as the GRAMMYs, is an award show presented by the United States Recording Academy whose mission is to "recognize excellence in the recording arts ... and ensure that music remains an indelible part of our culture" (Recording Academy, “About the Recording Academy”).

The Grammy Awards have been a pop culture phenomenon for decades, pulling in roughly 10 million views consistently for 40 years (The Wrap, “2025 Grammys Score 15.4 Million Viewers”). The widespread appeal of the awards has led to a commercial effect, known to be called the GRAMMY effect, which attributes large-scale commercial success, of an album, to winning a Album of the Year GRAMMY award (Vox, “Why the Grammys Have So Many Categories”).

Giacomo Negro at Emory University, along with colleagues, examined what happens to artists after receiving Grammy recognition. Their findings support the GRAMMY Effect: a nomination alone correlated with a jump of roughly 22 spots in Billboard peak chart position, while an actual win pushes that to about 27 (Negro, Kovács & Carroll, “What’s Next? Artists’ Music After Grammy Awards”).

Beyond their clear economic influence, the Grammy Awards hold major historical and sociological significance within the United States. In the 2006 paper Award Ceremony as an Arbiter of Commerce and Canon in the Popular Music Industry, Mary R Watson talks about how the Recording Academy positioned the Grammys as the “hallmark of peer recognition”, and that the Grammys positioned themselves, uniquely, as a mingle between "peer and popular recognition" making "them a significant arbiter of canon formation in the popular music industry" (Watson and Anand 2006).

Interestingly, the paper cites growing agendas of internal Recording Academy constituents to exert influence on award decisions formatively impacting legitimacy of genres in popular music industry. An early example of this is the first Grammy Awards (1958), which deliberately left out the high-Billboard charting rock-and-roll genre due to it being artistically 'inappropriate'. Within context, the Academy consisted of music elites (CEOs of Recording Studios, Radios) who considered rock-and-roll as a 'profane' genre (Watson and Anand 2006).

Additionally, Watson also states that the modern Awards have transformed to a status symbol within the music industry; with an analysis of the Billboard discourse finding that majority of the text units, between Artists, refer to the idea of winning an award translating to respect and reputation within the field (Watson and Anand 2006).

Ultimatley, the Grammys have a huge pop culture impact; posing a sociological, economical, and historical impact throughout the years. And today, the Grammy Awards voting process advocates for an unbiased decision based on merit. Specifically, the Voter's Code of Conduct states that a Voter must only be influenced by own analysis of merit, and stating they should not be suggested by "sales volume/popularity" or "company loyalties" (Recording Academy 2025).

This project questions this merit-based judging by doing an analysis, specifically, on quantitative album attributes relevant to commercial performance, critical reception, and musical characteristics of an album before it got nominated, and won, an Album of the Year award.

Contribution to Scholarship

Motivation behind the analysis

A Voter for the Grammy Awards, formally called the Recording Academy Voting Member, casts in the "Final Voting" phase - allowing these Voting Members to vote for three Fields in the genre Fields (in which they are advised to vote in their area of expertise) plus the four categories in the General Field (Recording Academy, 2025).

Because Album of the Year belongs to the General Field, every Voting Member is required to cast a vote for it. This naturally introduces high variability in voting outcomes, as not all voters share equal expertise.

Looking into how one can be a Record Academy Voting Member showed that it is an invite only group. Every year, the Recording Academy invites a "class" of new members into the Voting pool. The Recording Academy doesn't explicitly describe the qualifications to get an invitation, but rather states that Voting Members include "artists, producers, engineers, songwriters, and executive[s]" as a way to "recognize and celebrate the work of their peers" (Grammy.com 2025)

There does seem to be a red flag here, specifically between the official Academys' Voters Code of Conduct and the sample of people they chose to invite to be a Voting Member.

It would be naive to assume that top-level record executives are unaware of the GRAMMY Effect, or that they would ignore the potential financial gain that a win can produce by voting purely on artistic merit.

While we cannot directly prove voter intent, we can scrutinize loyalty and decision-making by examining correlations between sales volume or popularity and Album of the Year winners, as well as by analyzing musical characteristics to evaluate the expertise and priorities of the voting body.

Scoping Quantitative Album Attributes

We can question the merit-based judging by observing if there is a correlation between Album of the Year winners and "sales volume/popularity".

In this case, we need metrics pertaining to both "sales volume" and "popularity". Note that the two correlate each other, when an album has higher sales volume, it is more popular.

Sales volume is a bit tricky, especially because we have to use metrics that account for the streaming era. The Album-Equivalent Unit (AEU) does just that. In 2014, the Billboard music charts - an industry standard record charts - switched from a pure album-sales metric to a AEU metric (Billboard 2014). This new metric takes into account album-streams; with 10 song sales, or 1500 song streams contained within an album to one traditional album sale. This AEU metric, serves as a bridge between the present and the past, and seems to be an industry standard for today’s music-charts (Billboard 2014).

I talked briefly about how the Grammy Awards Voter’s Code of Conduct specifically says a voter must not vote on, among other things, "company loyalty". I made a hypothetical example with a record executive, and one of their signed artists, questioning the merit of their vote. While I acknowledged a correlation between that specific example is impossible to show, since the Voting Members list is not public, we can still question the merit with respect to a Voting Members "company loyalty" through other metrics.

Pitchfork is an American online music magazine, primarily known for its album reviews (Pitchfork 2021). Today, Pitchfork claims nearly seven million unique monthly visitors (Pitchfork 2025). In 2015, the American mass media company Condé Nast purchased Pitchfork, leading to a new creative direction and expanding opportunities for industry collaboration (Pitchfork 2015). Notably, in 2021, Condé Nast and Universal Music Group partnered in a joint initiative to “expand video reach for advertisers,” with the goal of allowing advertisers to access the combined “reach and influence of both companies (Universal Music Group 2021).”

Universal Music Group (UMG) is one of the “Big Three” companies controlling a significant portion of the United States music market (IFPI 2023). Notable modern artists under UMG include Taylor Swift, Kendrick Lamar, Drake, The Weeknd, Billie Eilish, and others. This direct connection and collaboration between Condé Nast and UMG prompts an investigation into whether a correlation exists between Pitchfork album review scores and Album of the Year winners. Such a correlation does not prove voter intent or label-based loyalty (and may be coincidental), but it raises the question of whether critical reviews and Grammy outcomes align more closely than expected.

Quantitative Album Attributes extends to the composition of musical characteristics as well. A song can contain quantitative attributes such as: Tempo (BPM), Key, Duration, and Loudness.

In a 2025 study titled Music Popularity Prediction Through Data Analysis of Music’s Characteristics, Jaehyun Kim developed a machine-learning model that achieved 90% accuracy in predicting song popularity based on quantitative audio features. His findings indicate that loudness, BPM, danceability, and valence directly correlate with a track’s popularity (Kim 2021). If similar attributes are found to correlate with Album of the Year winners, it calls into question the extent to which Voting Members prioritize artistic merit - and, accordingly, the Academy’s stated mission to "recognize excellence in the recording arts" .

Steps to execute the analysis

Fundementally, our analysis is three questions:

  1. Is there a correlation between an album’s Album-Equivalent Units (AEU)* and its likelihood of winning Album of the Year?
  2. Is there a correlation between Pitchfork review scores and Album of the Year outcomes?
  3. Is there a correlation between quantitative musical characteristics (BPM, loudness, danceability), that are shown to give a song mass appeal, and the album winning Album of the Year?

* Note: for our purpose, an album before the streaming era's AEU is just its pure sales. This is fine as AEU serves as a normalizing way to account for the streaming era.

Correlation is mathematically defined as the joint covariance between two random variables, divided by their products of their standard deviation (Ross 2019).

\documentclass{article} \usepackage{amsmath} \begin{document} \[ {\huge corr({X,Y}) = \frac{\operatorname{cov}(X, Y)}{\sigma_X \, \sigma_Y}} \] \end{document}

A random variable is a mapping from a sample space to the real numbers. Since our analysis deals with quantitative real-valued features, we can apply the standard covariance and standard deviation formulations directly when calculating joint covariance. (Agresti and Finlay 2009, 255–258)

The question then becomes: how do we map Album of the Year (AOTY) status to the real numbers? We define AOTY as an indicator/binary random variable, that is, 1 if album X wins, 0 otherwise.

This works, but it comes with a limitation. We are comparing continuous random features (AEUs, song characteristics, Pitchfork review scores) against a binary one. Traditional correlation formulas here flatten nuance, failing to represent the distributional differences between winners and non-winners. (Agresti and Finlay 2009, 255–258)

Statisticians have addressed this exact scenario. For a continuous feature measured against a binary outcome, the appropriate metric is the point-biserial correlation (Agresti and Finlay 2009, 255–258). Our analysis therefore partitions albums into winners and non-winners, then compares the mean value of each quantitative feature across groups.

What we get is a coefficent, rpb, which ranges from -1 to 1 with extremitys indicating a perfect negative correlation, or a perfect postive correlation, respectively.

\documentclass{article} \usepackage{amsmath} \begin{document} \[ \huge{r_{pb} = \frac{\mu_{1} - \mu_{0}}{s_{n}} \sqrt{\frac{n_{1} n_{0}}{n^{2}}}} \] \end{document}

The formula essentially tells us to take the mean value of our continuous feature X (AEU, song characteristics, Pitchfork review scores) among winners, minus the mean among non-winners. We then divide this difference by the standard deviation of X, scaled by a factor that accounts for sample balance across groups. Specifically, the denominator includes the standard deviation of X multiplied by the square root of the product of group sizes divided by the total number of data points squared (Agresti and Finlay 2009, 255-258).

This allows us to evaluate how strongly higher feature values, X, are associated with winning.

Data and Methods

Since our analysis deals with three questions, we need 3 "master" data sets. These data sets can be an aggregate of existing data sets, and traditional historical sources.

Historic Album of the Year Winners, and Nominees

Every one of our datasets will need Album of the Year Winners, and Nominees for each Grammy Awards. The Official Grammys Website does infact have this, but we would have to scrape the data, as nominees are not in the same place as the winners.

Album Equivalent Units

The first dataset we have to construct is the AEU of each Album of the Year nominee and winners. This will give us exactly what we need to programatically get our rpb coefficent.

The challenge is to locate the date of each Grammy Awards historically, then measure a fixed-timeframe AEU for every nominated album. This gives us a fairer comparison, since each album is evaluated over the same relative period leading up to the ceremony.

The Recording Academy has already approached eligibility in a similar time-bounded way. They enforce a release window that determines which albums qualify for a given ceremony, commonly cited as September 1st through August 31st in recent years (Recording Academy 2025).

The remaining obstacle is data reliability, especially for older releases. Because of this, our time window needs to be selected carefully and possibly adjusted across eras.

Since 1991, the Billboard 200 has operated on weekly sales logs collected through Nielsen SoundScan (now Luminate), meaning album performance has been tracked at the point of sale and converted into weekly unit counts (Billboard 2021). This is great, but leaves a gap from 1958 - 1991. What we do have, pretty consistently, from the late 1960s is weekly charts.

Regression on Chart Position to Estimated Sales

Having access to a Billboard PRO account gives us access to all historical archived charts, from the companys' inception. This is the data we want to estimate album sales for. What we would do is, first normalize our 1991 - present data, and train a regression model on the data. We would have to experiment with different regression models, but we would want a linear model and a non-linear model where we can compare baseline metrics.

Say we have a simple linear regression model (Agresti and Finlay 2009, 255–258):

\documentclass{article} \usepackage{amsmath} \begin{document} \[ \huge{y = \textbf{w}^{T}\textbf{x} + b} \] \end{document}

The goal of the model is to learn the weights w = [w1, w2] by minimizing the squared error between predictions and the true sales values (Agresti and Finlay 2009, 255–258).

Here, y represents weekly unit sales, and x encodes chart position and album release date. We will run a standard 80 20 train test split on the SoundScan era data, evaluate performance, and select the model that yields the strongest results.

Our baseline evaluation metric will be R^2. It gives us a direct measure of how strongly our model explains variance in weekly sales.

Once trained, the model lets us infer sales from historical weekly chart positions. Because charting is consistent across eras, we will estimate early sales using a four week window of inferred weekly units and average them to produce a stable sales signal.

By including release date as an input, we expect the model to detect the underlying trend that weekly sales scale upward in later decades as access to music explodes.

To summarize we have access to the following information:

  • From 2014, to the present, we have weekly unit sales in AEU for each charting album
  • From 1991 to 2014, we have pure-sale weekly unit sales for each charting album. This is fine, as AEU is trying to account for the "streaming era" , i.e. AEU is a data normalization.
  • From 1958 to 1990, we only consistently have weekly charts. For Albums that do not have archival information on their weekly unit sales, we will use the model to estimate them.

The reason why we are not using the model consistently, is because we are confident that there are albums within the 1958 to 1990 timeframe that do have weekly unit sales. This is due to their popularity, and prestiege, that digital historians have more care in preserving the data.

Pitchfork Reviews

The obvious limitation is Pitchfork’s age. The site launched in 1996, but its modern, standardized review system did not solidify until around 2009. That leaves a large portion of Grammy history with no consistent scoring baseline.

The tradeoff is that we can still analyze whether Pitchfork’s influence increases over time. If higher review scores trend alongside Album of the Year winners in later years, it suggests a potential alignment between critical reception and Grammy outcomes.

The first step is to pull historical reviews using digital archiving platforms such as archive.org. This gives us access to older review pages that may not appear in the live index.

We will only include reviews that were published before nomination announcements for the relevant Grammy year. That ensures we are measuring critical reception prior to voting, not after.

This workflow is manual. For every year from 2009 forward we need to locate the nomination release date, then filter archived Pitchfork pages to include only reviews posted before that point.

Song Characteristics

As stated in the previous section, song characteristics such as loudness, BPM, dancebility, and valence directly correlate with a tracks popularity.

Since an Album is an accumilation of songs, we can average each of the song charactistics and get album characteristics.

The great thing is, the paper that was written showing the correlation to popularity from those metrics specifically got their metrics from Spotify. This means that the Spotify Application Programming Interface (API) enables us to easily get formatted data for each album, each song within the album, and each song characteristic.

Our data engineering here would just to build a script that will automate this for each song within each Album, and get an aggregate. We can then perform the correlation as described in the previous section.
